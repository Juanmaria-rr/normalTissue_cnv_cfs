scDNA-seq QC: levels 1 and 2 — method, decisions and examples

2026-10-29 · @u_wkfYxeifhjCpb4YVu5LdHg

Summary and scope

QC has two levels. Level 1 decides whether each cell's library is valid and how many usable reads it has. Level 2 decides whether its copy number (CN) profile is reliable as a source of breakends. In the 20-cell test, every cell passed level 1, but only 7 of 20 have enough depth for an integer fit at 200 kb (15 of 20 at 500 kb and 18 of 20 at 1 Mb).

Update of 29 October (after this document was written). Breakends are computed from the relative CN, not the absolute CN. Each segment is classified by its ratio to the cell's median (gain > 1.3, loss < 0.7), and segments shorter than 1 Mb are dropped. The absolute conversion only rescales segments and does not move change points. In this near-diploid population, a ratio threshold is equivalent to a one-copy step, and rounding to integers adds its own errors. Consequences:

  • depth is judged with the diploid threshold;

  • the unfiltered absolute fit is used only as a flag (ploidy ≥ 2.6, or disagreement with --filters powered);

  • manual curation becomes an audit of ~100 cells;

  • the integer-CN breakpoint definition of level 2.5 is replaced by this one.

Underlying question. We are looking for CNA breakends enriched at common fragile sites (CFS). So every QC step is judged by one thing: whether it can create false breakends or lose real ones, and whether it does so in a way correlated with CFS (late replication, large gene-poor regions).

Data in the examples. All numbers and plots come from the real 20-cell test of 29 October 2026: 4 randomly chosen cells (seed 1) from each pilot donor (P07, P08, P18, P20 and P36). Alignment ran on AWS HealthOmics (run 7883373) and CN locally with the same container (cnv-cfs/copynumber). With 20 cells the examples illustrate the method; thresholds are calibrated on the full pilot (9,181 cells).

The workflow at a glance

Under each step is what is measured and the section where it is explained. Level 1 is computed once per cell. Level 2 is repeated for each bin size, and a cell can pass at 500 kb but not at 200 kb.

Level 1 — Outline: steps, criteria and rationale

Steps 1 to 5 do not exclude cells: they either detect pipeline failures or discard reads. Only step 6 decides whether a cell is included, and it decides separately for each bin size.

Level 1.1 — Input reads: R1 only and concatenation per cell

Each cell enters as a single read per fragment (R1), pooling all its runs. In the 20 test cells, the reads entering the pipeline match ENA's read_count exactly, including the 6 cells with two runs.

What is done. Each run is extracted with fasterq-dump --split-3. From paired runs _1.fastq is kept; from single-end runs, the only read. Runs of the same cell (same donor, chip, well and assay) are concatenated before trimming and alignment.

Why R1 only. QDNAseq binReadCounts() counts reads, not fragments. With paired BAMs, both mates of a fragment almost always fall in the same bin. That doubles the count without adding independent observations, and breaks two things:

  • The threshold of 15 reads per bin per copy (nrpbpcc) would admit cells with ~7.5 real fragments.

  • The variance would no longer be Poisson on the count, which is the basis of the noise metric of level 2.4.

In addition, the batch mixes single-end and paired runs (table). Using R1 only makes them comparable.

Why concatenate. About 7,354 cells of the study were sequenced in two runs. The second is the same library, sequenced again for more depth, often on another instrument. Treating each run as a cell would count the same cell twice, with half the depth in each. Concatenating before markdup has another advantage: PCR duplicates appearing in both runs are detected together (level 1.4).

Real example. Cell P08_chip1_co_r0c11 has a 46 bp single-end run (SRR25851362, 65,159 reads) and a 58 bp paired run (SRR30469041, 217,096 fragments). 282,255 reads went in, which is the exact sum.

Level 1.2 — Trimming: no hard trim, and cutadapt for the Tn5 adapter

Nothing is trimmed from the 5' end. Only read-through into the Tn5 adapter at the 3' end is removed (cutadapt -a CTGTCTCTTATACACATCT -m 20). Reads shorter than 20 bp are discarded: in the 20 cells there were between 0 and 13 per cell.

What the starting pipeline proposed. macintyrelab/scDNAseq offers two optional trims:

  • --hardtrim3 read.length-14: removes the first 14 bp of each read. It is meant for protocols with a pre-amplification adapter at the 5' end.

  • cutadapt -a <sequence>: removes a sequence overrepresented at the 3' end.

The libraries of this study are ACT, i.e. Tn5 tagmentation without pre-amplification. Before deciding, we checked one run of each instrument and layout type.

Evidence.

Run (type)Reads with the Tn5 mosaic end (12-mer)Most frequent leading 14-mer
SRR25851362 (NextSeq SE 46 bp)7 / 65,159 (0.01%)ATTCCATTCCATTC: satellite (90 reads)
SRR25848185 (HiSeq SE 50 bp)972 / 184,997 (0.53%)CACACACACACACA: CA repeat (387)
SRR25825846 (NovaSeq PE 50 bp, R1)3,358 / 814,543 (0.41%)CCTGTAATCCCAGC: Alu (1,270)
SRR30471038 (NextSeq PE 54 bp, R1)15,948 / 574,816 (2.77%)CACACACACACACA: CA repeat (854)

No run has a constant prefix: the most frequent 14-mers are genomic repeats, and the most common one is below 0.2% of reads. A pre-amplification adapter would appear in almost 100%. Read-through into the mosaic end does appear: these are fragments shorter than the read.

Why it matters for CN.

  • The 14 bp hard trim would shorten 46 bp reads to 32 bp for no reason. At 32 bp the fraction of reads with MAPQ ≥ 37 drops, and the loss is not uniform: it concentrates in repetitive regions and segmental duplications, where many CFS are.

  • The untrimmed adapter stays in the read. bwa mem usually removes it by soft-clipping, but with very short fragments the read can map badly or with low MAPQ. The effect varies between runs (0.01–2.8%), so it ends up as a batch effect.

  • -m 20 exists because cutadapt leaves empty or nearly empty reads when the fragment is shorter than the adapter, and bwa fails or places them at random. Upstream sets no minimum.

A caveat when reading cutadapt reports. cutadapt counts as "with adapter" any match of 3 bp or more at the 3' end (its default minimum overlap). That is why it reports ~6% of reads with adapter, while the full mosaic end is much rarer. Those random 3–5 bp trims cost little mappability.

Limitation. One run per instrument and layout type was checked, not all of them. If a run had another adapter, it would show as a drop in mapping rate at level 1.3, which is monitored in every cell.

Level 1.3 — Alignment and mapping quality (MAPQ)

98.2–98.8% of reads map to hg19 in the 20 cells. The loss that matters is not unmapped reads (1.4% of the total) but reads with MAPQ < 37: between 20 and 28% of non-duplicate reads.

What is done. bwa mem 0.7.17 in single-end mode against hg19 (UCSC; chrM, chr1–22, X and Y; no alternative contigs or decoys) → samtools sort → samtools markdup -s → index and flagstat. These are the versions pinned by the group's pipeline. Low-MAPQ reads are not removed from the BAM: QDNAseq does not count them (binReadCounts, minMapq = 37 by default).

Why hg19. The whole downstream chain is built on hg19: the QDNAseq bin annotations (GC, mappability, blacklist, residuals), the replication timing of sWGS_relativeCN and the enrichment script. Moving to GRCh38 would require regenerating all those annotations.

Why MAPQ ≥ 37. With 46–58 bp reads, a read in a repeat or a segmental duplication can be placed in several copies. MAPQ ≥ 37 keeps the ones with a unique position.

  • Con: the filter is not uniform along the genome. It mostly removes reads from repetitive regions and segmental duplications, which is where many CFS and many structural breakpoints fall.

  • How it is compensated: the QDNAseq annotations include each bin's mappability (computed with 50 bp reads). Poorly mappable bins or bins with high residuals in controls are excluded (applyFilters), and the rest are corrected for GC and mappability.

  • Consequence for CFS: a CFS in a poorly mappable region does not generate false breakends, but it does reduce the ability to see them. When permuting, the null has to account for which bins are usable (level 2.6).

What is monitored. A mapping rate < 90% would be flagged as an anomalous library (contamination, unknown adapter or wrong reference). It is not used to exclude, but as an alarm that something in the pipeline is wrong.

Limitation. The mappability of the QDNAseq annotations was computed with 50 bp reads; our reads range from 46 to 58 bp. The 46 bp reads (NextSeq SE) have slightly less mappability than the annotation assumes, which can leave a residual batch bias in repetitive bins.

Level 1.4 — Duplicates

Duplicates are marked in the BAM with samtools markdup -s and QDNAseq excludes them when counting. They are not used to exclude cells. In three of the five donors they match the published values; P08 comes out somewhat higher and P20 doubles the expected value: 36–46% vs 16% in the paper.

Data flow. Input: data/pilot/align_qc_test20.tsv, data/metadata/paper_scdna_qc.tsv (Lin et al. 2024 Suppl. Table 2) → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/dup_vs_paper.tsv. Shown here: per-cell duplicate % of the 20 test cells next to each donor's duplicate rate in the paper.

What is done. After sorting by position, markdup marks as duplicates the reads with the same chromosome, the same 5' position and the same strand, and keeps one per group. Since we only use R1, deduplication is single-end.

Why they have to be removed. In a single cell, each read should be a different fragment of the ~2 copies of the genome. A PCR duplicate repeats the same fragment: it adds no new observation, but it adds to the bin count. Without deduplication:

  • depth looks higher than it is (cells passing powered without deserving it);

  • the variance between bins stops being Poisson, because one fragment amplified 10 times puts 10 reads in one bin;

  • a highly amplified fragment can mimic a one-bin focal gain.

Does R1 deduplication overestimate duplicates in paired runs? Yes, in theory: two different fragments that start at the same position are marked as one, even if they end at different places. In practice, with N reads spread over about 2 × 2.8 × 10⁹ stranded positions, the fraction of random collisions is of the order of N / 5.6 × 10⁹. With 1 M reads that is ~0.02%. What we see (10–46%) are real library duplicates.

Reading the plot.

  • P18 and P07 DNA-only (9–17%) match the paper (12.7% and 15.2%).

  • The co-assay has lower-complexity libraries. Within P07, the co-assay cell has 43% vs 12–17% for the DNA-only cells. P36, all co-assay, is at 36–43%, the same as in the paper (38%). This does not change the nature of the data (they are WGS), but it does mean fewer usable reads per sequenced read. That is why the assay type is kept as a covariate.

  • P20 departs. It is not saturation: it also happens in cells with 69 k reads. It is not collisions either. With 4 cells we cannot tell whether the paper computes the rate differently (for example, by pairs or only on the first run) or whether these cells are atypical. Pending the full pilot (2,992 P20 cells).

Limitation. Duplication is an indicator of library complexity, not of CN quality. A cell with 45% duplicates and enough usable reads is perfectly valid. What decides whether a cell is included is its usable reads (level 1.5).

Level 1.5 — Usable reads and depth threshold

What decides whether a cell can be fitted to integer CN is its usable reads: primary, mapped, MAPQ ≥ 37 and non-duplicate. These are exactly the reads QDNAseq counts. In the test they were between 39% and 69% of sequenced reads, and only 7 of 20 cells reach the threshold for 200 kb bins.

Data flow. Input: align workflow QC files (qc/.cutadapt.txt, qc/.flagstat.txt, qc/.markdup.txt, bam/.bam) and ENA counts (data/metadata/cells.tsv); QDNAseq bin annotations → Script: scripts/02_align/summarize_align_qc.py, scripts/00_metadata/count_usable_bins.R → Output: data/pilot/align_qc_test20.tsv, data/metadata/usable_bins.tsv. Shown here: usable reads per test cell against the diploid depth threshold computed from the usable-bin counts.

The threshold. fitting_absCN requires at least 15 reads per bin per chromosome copy (nrpbpcc) for a fit to be considered powered:

\text{reads needed} = \frac{15 \times \text{ploidy} \times \text{usable bins}}{0.91}

For a diploid cell, with the usable bins of each size (autosomes, after applyFilters):

Data flow. Input: QDNAseq bin annotations (200 kb from external/sWGS_relativeCN/bin.annotations; 500 kb and 1 Mb from QDNAseq.hg19) → Script: scripts/00_metadata/count_usable_bins.R → Output: data/metadata/usable_bins.tsv. Shown here: usable autosomal bins per bin size and the reads needed for a diploid cell.

Why 15. With Poisson counts of mean λ per copy, telling 2 copies from 3 means telling λ = 30 from λ = 45 reads in a bin. With λ = 15 per copy, the difference between CN 2 and CN 3 (15 reads) is about 2.7 times the standard deviation at CN 2 (√30 ≈ 5.5). That is enough to classify individual bins reasonably before segmentation. Below it, counting noise swamps one-copy steps (see the depth figure at level 2.2).

The 0.91 constant does not hold. The 0.91 of fitting_absCN assumes that 91% of sequenced reads end up counted. It is calibrated on bulk data, with few duplicates. Here the real fraction is 39–69%, because of duplicates (9–46%) and low MAPQ (13–21% of the total). Two consequences:

  • The counts of cells "that pass" computed from ENA's read_count (in the README) are a fairly optimistic upper bound.

  • Since powered is computed on QDNAseq's total.reads (reads already counted in bins), the filter itself is correct. What was wrong was the earlier estimate from ENA.

Decision taken: 200 kb as the primary bin. It is comparable to the paper's 220 kb CopyKit bins and gives the best breakend resolution for CFS (0.5 to several Mb). 500 kb and 1 Mb are sensitivity analyses. The cost is in the plot: fewer cells, selected towards the deep batches. All of P08 passes, and almost none of the P20 and P36 co-assay.

If aneuploid cells systematically had fewer or more usable reads than diploid ones (for example, because of nuclear size or library complexity), the depth filter would change the observed aneuploid fraction. This will be checked in the pilot by comparing the depth of aneuploid and diploid cells within each donor.

Level 2 — Outline: steps, criteria and rationale

Order matters. Ploidy (step 2) sets the scale on which powered, clonality and breakpoints depend, so an error there carries over to everything else. The four excluding filters (steps 3, 5, 6 and 7) are applied separately for each bin size, and the output table records the first reason for exclusion.

Level 2.1 — Relative CN with QDNAseq: bins, filters, correction and segmentation

Relative CN is computed with an unmodified sWGS_relativeCN (QDNAseq 1.26 + QDNAseqmod 1.21), with the parameters of the upstream example: --undo.SD 1 --cnaware NO --mappability YES --reptiming NO. The same cell is processed at 200 kb, 500 kb and 1 Mb. A real event appears at all three sizes; noise changes with bin size.

P08_chip1_co_r71c48 (1.2 M usable reads). Grey: CN of each bin; blue: segment CN at the chosen ploidy. Vertical lines separate chromosomes. The chr6q gain (68–95 Mb, CN 3) appears at all three bin sizes. Dispersion between bins falls as the bin grows, because each bin counts more reads.

Data flow. Input: 20-cell test outputs (HealthOmics align run 7883373 BAMs; copynumber test relCN/absCN): relCN/_kb/*.rds, absCN/_kb/ → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/fig_profile_bins_P08_r71c48.png. Shown here: the CN profile of P08_chip1_co_r71c48 at three bin sizes.

The steps, in order.

  1. Bins. The genome is split into fixed windows. At 200 kb the annotation shipped with sWGS_relativeCN is used; at 500 kb and 1 Mb, those of QDNAseq.hg19. Each bin carries its GC, its mappability (50 bp reads), whether it is blacklisted and its residual in controls.

  2. Counting (binReadCounts): reads per bin with MAPQ ≥ 37, excluding duplicates and secondary alignments.

  3. Filters (applyFilters(residual = TRUE, blacklist = TRUE)): bins in the ENCODE/1000G blacklist and bins with an anomalous residual in control genomes are removed. These are bins that give signal even when CN is normal. 12,017 usable autosomal bins remain at 200 kb, 4,407 at 500 kb and 1,942 at 1 Mb.

  4. GC and mappability correction (estimateCorrection + correctBins): a two-dimensional loess fit of the count against GC and mappability, per cell. The count is divided by the fitted value and rescaled to the median, so it stays in read space.

  5. Outlier smoothing (smoothOutlierBins): isolated bins far from their neighbours are pulled towards the local median.

  6. Segmentation (segmentBins, DNAcopy CBS with a square-root transform, undo.SD = 1): change points are searched for. Two adjacent segments are merged if their means differ by less than 1 standard deviation.

Why each choice.

ParameterValueWhyRisk
--mappabilityYESCorrects read loss in poorly mappable bins, frequent near CFSOver-correcting bins with very low mappability
--cnawareNOCN-aware correction (grouping bins by CN level before the loess) is meant for tumours with many changes; here most cells are diploidIn highly aneuploid cells, the global loess can absorb part of a real change correlated with GC
--reptimingNOUpstream only corrects replication timing at 30 and 50 kb; it does not apply at 200 kbThe S-phase signature stays uncorrected: it is handled as QC (level 2.6)
--undo.SD1The value upstream uses with low-noise samplesWith low depth, spurious segments are generated (levels 2.4 and 2.5)

Why the square-root transform. It stabilises the Poisson variance: if the count is x ~ Poisson(λ), √x has a variance of about 1/4 regardless of λ. Without it, CBS would see more noise in bins with more reads (gains) and would segment gains and losses differently.

Limitation. The loess correction and the filters were calibrated on bulk data with 50 bp reads. In single cells with uneven amplification, residual GC waves can remain, which the segmenter can turn into segments. The noise metric (level 2.4) and the replication-timing metric (level 2.6) are there to detect those cases.

Level 2.2 — Absolute CN and the powered flag

Relative CN is converted into integer CN by searching for the ploidy that best fits the segments onto integers. With low depth, that fit gives meaningless results. That is why each fit carries the powered flag: whether the cell has at least 15 nrpbpcc at that ploidy. In the test, cells without powered at 200 kb were given ploidies of 1.4, 1.6 and 3.1, whereas all of them are diploid at 500 kb.

Top, P18_chip1_dna_r55c17 (602 k usable reads, powered): the whole genome at CN 2 with compact dispersion. Bottom, P20_chip1_co_r40c13 (33 k, 18 times fewer, not powered): bins scatter from 0 to more than 6, and the fit chooses ploidy 1.4 with segments between 1.3 and 1.8 that correspond to no integer.

Data flow. Input: 20-cell test outputs (HealthOmics align run 7883373 BAMs; copynumber test relCN/absCN): relCN, absCN → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/fig_profile_depth_200kb.png. Shown here: 200 kb profiles of a deep (powered) and a shallow (not powered) test cell.

What pureSamples_absCN.R does. For each cell it scans a grid of ploidy (1.4 to 8, in steps of 0.1) and purity (0.90 to 1.00, in steps of 0.01). For each pair:

  1. it scales the segmented CN to absolute: CN = (segment depth / depth per copy) adjusted for purity;

  2. it computes the clonality: the mean of (CN − round(CN))² over bins (level 2.3);

  3. it flags whether the fit is powered, whether it leaves more than 10 Mb below 0.8 copies (LOHok), and whether any essential gene has no copies (essentialok).

For each ploidy it keeps the purity with the lowest clonality. Then it chooses the ploidy with the lowest clonality among those passing the given filter (--filters powered).

What --filters powered does not do. If no ploidy passes the filter, the script does not discard the cell: it assigns the best unfiltered fit. Shallow cells reach absoluteCN.rds like any other. That is why our QC reads powered from the chosen fit and uses it to exclude, separately for each bin size.

Why not trust a fit without powered. The fit looks for the ploidy that leaves segments closest to integers. If noise is large, the segmenter produces segments whose value depends on the noise, and there is always some ploidy that accommodates them reasonably. Clonality is low, but by chance. In the figure, the bottom cell has clonality 0.0085. That is higher than the deep cell's (0.0005), but about 20 times lower than the bad fit of level 2.3 (0.18), even though its profile is clearly unusable.

Purity in single cells. A cell has no "purity". The 0.90–1.00 grid leaves a margin for contamination by ambient DNA in the well and for scaling errors. In practice the chosen fit is almost always at or near 1.00.

ProsCons
Objective filter linking depth and integer-CN resolutionThe 15 nrpbpcc threshold comes from bulk; it has not been validated in single cells
Evaluated per bin size: a cell can be included at 500 kb but not at 200 kbFor near-tetraploid cells the threshold doubles, so polyploid cells are lost first

Limitation. For cells near the threshold, powered depends on the chosen ploidy, and the ploidy depends on depth. The ambiguous ones (for example, 2 vs 4) are precisely the ones we send to manual review in the Shiny app.

Level 2.3 — Clonality, and a flaw in the powered filter

Clonality measures how far segments depart from integers at the chosen ploidy. Reviewing it revealed a flaw: --filters powered can choose a wrong ploidy. For P36_co_r51c33 it chose 1.6 (clonality 0.18), although the best fit is 2.0 (0.0049), only because 1.6 is the only ploidy that passes the depth threshold.

Data flow. Input: 20-cell test outputs (HealthOmics align run 7883373 BAMs; copynumber test relCN/absCN): absCN/_200kb/_resfitting.txt → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/clonality_curves.tsv. Shown here: clonality versus ploidy (best purity per ploidy) for two example cells, 200 kb.

Definition. For a candidate ploidy and purity, with each bin's absolute CN (from its segment):

\text{clonality} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( CN_i - \operatorname{round}(CN_i) \right)^2

It is 0 if all segments fall on integers. The theoretical maximum is 0.25, when all fall halfway. In the well-fitted test cells it is between 0.0005 and 0.007.

Why it is useful. In a single cell, every segment should have an integer number of copies, because there are no subclones within a cell. High clonality means the scale is wrong: either the chosen ploidy is incorrect, or the profile is so noisy that segments do not reflect real levels. That is why it is the metric that sends cells to manual review.

The minima at 2, 3, 4 and 5 are the ploidy ambiguity. A nearly flat profile fits CN 2 across the genome just as well as CN 3 or 4 across the genome. The curve has a minimum at each integer, and the lowest is usually at 2 by a small margin (0.0009 vs 0.0021 at 3 and 0.0037 at 4 for P36_co_r65c23). This is the "2 vs 4" case we decided to review by hand in the Shiny app.

The flaw. The depth threshold is 15 × ploidy × bins / 0.91, so it falls when ploidy falls. P36_co_r51c33 has 319 k usable reads:

  • at ploidy 2.0 it would need 396 k → not powered;

  • at ploidy 1.6 it needs 15 × 1.6 × 12,017 / 0.91 ≈ 317 k → powered by a hair.

--filters powered keeps the powered fits and chooses the one with the lowest clonality among them. There is only one, so it chooses 1.6, with clonality 0.18, almost the worst possible value. And since our QC read powered from the chosen fit, the cell passed the filter with a wrong absolute CN across the whole genome: it appears with ploidy 1.6 and 3.7% "non-diploid" bins that are an artefact.

The same cell at 200 kb and at 500 kb. At 200 kb segments sit at 1.5–1.8, between integers; at 500 kb, where it is powered at ploidy 2, everything falls on 2.

Data flow. Input: 20-cell test outputs (HealthOmics align run 7883373 BAMs; copynumber test relCN/absCN): relCN, absCN → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/fig_profile_badfit_P36_r51c33.png. Shown here: the 200 kb profile of P36_chip1_co_r51c33 under the filtered (1.6) and best (2.0) ploidy fits.

Fix (decided on 29 October). Separate the two decisions:

  1. Depth = diploid threshold (15 × 2 × bins / 0.91), independent of the fit, because breakends come from the relative CN. P36_co_r51c33 is excluded at 200 kb (319 k < 396 k).

  2. Ploidy of the best unfiltered fit (what fitting_absCN calls uploidy): as a flag only. If it is ≥ 2.6, the cell is not near-diploid and goes to review or leaves the ratio-based analysis.

  3. Alarm when the filtered and unfiltered ploidies disagree (fit_disagree): it indicates a cell near the threshold.

The QC script can do this by reading <cell>_resfitting.txt, without touching upstream.

Level 2.4 — Noise: excess over Poisson noise

We measure noise as how much the dispersion between neighbouring bins exceeds what counting noise alone would explain. In the 20 cells it is between 0.95 and 1.17, i.e. almost all of the noise is counting noise. Classic MAPD, by contrast, ranges from 0.17 to 1.04 and is explained mostly by depth.

Data flow. Shown here: per cell and bin: classic MAPD and noise_excess against reads per bin per copy.

Definition. For each pair of neighbouring bins on the same chromosome, with x the corrected count (in read space) and s the mean of its segment:

z_i = \frac{x_{i+1} - x_i}{\sqrt{s_{i+1} + s_i}}, \qquad \text{noise\_excess} = \operatorname{MAD}(z) \times 1.4826

If each bin's count is Poisson with mean s, the difference between two neighbours has variance sᵢ + sᵢ₊₁, so z has standard deviation 1. The MAD is robust to the few pairs that cross a real breakpoint. A value of 1.3 means 30% more dispersion than depth explains: uneven amplification, degraded library or incomplete GC correction.

Why not a fixed MAPD. MAPD mixes two things: counting noise, which depends on how many reads each bin has, and extra technical noise. In the left plot, MAPD is almost a function of depth. A fixed threshold (for example, MAPD > 0.4) would exclude almost all cells below ~5 nrpbpcc, and those cells come mostly from specific batches (P20 and P36 co-assay). It would be a batch filter disguised as a quality filter.

Why not relative per donor. A median + 3 MAD threshold within each donor has the opposite problem. If all cells of a donor are noisy, their normal values pass anyway, and the threshold changes from donor to donor, which makes results hard to compare.

Decision taken. Use noise_excess with a threshold k calibrated on the P18 diploid cells, the noise reference donor, plus an absolute safety cap. k is fixed on the pilot before looking at features.

Reading the example. No cell exceeds 1.17. The two highest are P07_co_r41c34 (1.168), the one with the 3-bin segment at CN 8 (level 2.5), and P08_co_r71c48 (1.152), the one with the chr6q gain. With 20 cells there is no clearly noisy one: the pilot will show what the tail of the distribution looks like.

ProsCons
Independent of depth by construction: one criterion for every batchAssumes Poisson: legitimate biological overdispersion (for example, S phase) also raises it
Robust to real breakpoints (neighbours + MAD)GC correction rescales the counts, so the equivalence with Poisson is approximate; that is why k is calibrated on data and not fixed at 1

Limitation. With very few reads per bin (< 2 nrpbpcc), the normal approximation of the Poisson difference fails and the MAD of nearly discrete values is unstable. In practice those cells already fail powered.

Level 2.5 — Breakpoints

A breakpoint is a change of integer CN between two adjacent usable bins on the same chromosome. It is the unit later crossed with CFS, so any false breakpoint is direct noise in the result. In the test, the two cases with breakpoints illustrate the two problems to solve: very short segments and values halfway between integers.

Definition we use. On the absolute segments rounded to the nearest integer: breakpoints = number of pairs of neighbouring bins, within a chromosome, whose integer CN changes. Changes between chromosomes or across bins excluded by filters are not counted.

Example 1: a short, intense segment.

P07_chip1_co_r41c34 at 200 kb: a 3-bin segment (chr4:18.0–18.6 Mb) at CN 8 generates 2 breakpoints. The cell is not powered at 200 kb (7.2 nrpbpcc) and the segment does not appear at 500 kb or 1 Mb. It is most likely a local artefact (a few amplified fragments or a poorly filtered region), not a real amplification.

Example 2: the same real event, but the number of breakpoints changes with the bin. P08_chip1_co_r71c48 has 2 breakpoints at 200 kb and at 1 Mb (the chr6q gain, 67.5–95 Mb, CN 2.92 vs 1.96), but 4 at 500 kb. The 2 extra come from a segment at chr9:112.0–116.5 Mb with CN 2.55. That value sits halfway between 2 and 3 and is rounded to 3. A segment like that, present at only one bin size, is almost certainly segmentation noise.

Why > 15 like the paper. Lin et al. 2024 exclude cells with more than 15 breakpoints because those profiles are usually dividing or degraded cells, not clonal aneuploidies. Adopting it keeps us comparable. Their count, however, is done on CopyKit segments (220 kb) and not on integer CN, so our 15 is not exactly the same threshold. We will review it with the pilot distribution.

Limitation. The precision of each breakend is ±1 bin (±200 kb at 200 kb), and worse when the segmenter shifts the change point in noisy regions. That is why the enrichment analysis uses adaptive windows around each feature and not exact overlaps.

Level 2.6 — S-phase signature and replication timing

A cell that was replicating its DNA has more copies in early-replicating regions. It is the artefact that most resembles what we are looking for, because CFS are late-replicating. In the test no cell shows it: the correlation with replication timing ranges from −0.023 to +0.083.

Data flow. Input: 20-cell test outputs (HealthOmics align run 7883373 BAMs; copynumber test relCN/absCN): relCN/_200kb/*.rds, plus external/sWGS_relativeCN/bin.annotations/bins_with_replicationTime_50kb.tsv → Script: scripts/docs/qc_doc_data.R → Output: docs/qc_levels_1_2/rt_deciles.tsv. Shown here: mean relative CN of each test cell by replication-timing decile, 200 kb.

The mechanism. In mid S phase, early-replicating regions already have 4 copies and late ones still 2. The CN profile "waves" following replication timing, in blocks of several Mb. The segmenter cuts those waves and creates breakends at the boundaries between early and late domains. Since CFS lie in large late domains, those breakends would fall near CFS more than expected by chance: a false enrichment with exactly the shape of the result we are looking for.

How it is measured.

  • rt_rho: Spearman correlation, bin by bin, between relative CN (bin / cell median) and the bin's replication timing. The 50 kb RT values of sWGS_relativeCN are averaged within each bin; a high value means early replication. A cell in S phase gives a positive ρ.

  • To visualise it, the plot groups bins into RT deciles and averages the relative CN of each decile.

Reading the example.

  • The 15 cells with ≥ 3 nrpbpcc are flat: at most a 6% difference between deciles. The one that rises most towards early is P07_co_r41c34 (+5%, ρ = 0.083), far from what S phase would look like.

  • The largest deviations come from 3 of the 5 cells with very low depth, and they go the other way: more signal in late regions. P36_co_r44c36 goes from 1.24 in the latest decile to 1.07 in the earliest. This is not S phase. The most likely explanation is a residual of GC correction with low counts, since late domains are GC-poor. Those cells fail powered anyway.

Decision taken.

  1. Exclude cells with an S-phase signature from the main analysis. The rt_rho cut-off is calibrated on P18, using the right tail of its distribution as reference.

  2. Sensitivity analysis with those cells included.

  3. Permutation null stratified by RT. Random breakends are placed respecting the late/mid/early proportion of the observed ones, and only in usable bins. The test then asks whether there are more breakends in CFS than expected for their replication timing, not whether breakends fall in late regions.

Why exclude rather than correct. sWGS_relativeCN only corrects replication timing at 30 and 50 kb, and the correction is a loess of CN against RT. Applied to a cell with real losses in late domains, it can erase part of the true signal, which in this case is precisely the signal we are looking for.

Pros of excluding + RT-stratified nullCons
Removes the most direct confounder and controls the residual in the testA cell with real CNAs in early domains can give ρ > 0 and be excluded by mistake
The sensitivity analysis shows how much the decision weighsCells at the end of S phase (almost everything replicated) have low ρ and escape the filter

Limitation. The RT comes from cell lines, not from breast epithelium. Late domains are fairly well conserved across tissues, but not entirely, and some CFS are tissue-specific.

General limitations.

  • Everything above comes from 20 cells. The numbers illustrate the method; they are not estimates of anything.

  • The annotations (mappability, blacklist, RT) come from bulk data and cell lines, not from single breast cells.

  • Excluding by depth is excluding by batch. We compensate by reporting what is retained per donor, assay type and instrument, and with sensitivity analyses at 500 kb and 1 Mb.

  • Breakend precision: ±1 bin. It is comparable to the size of many CFS, so enrichment is diluted but not invented.
